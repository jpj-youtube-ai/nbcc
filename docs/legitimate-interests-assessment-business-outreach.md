## Why this document exists

UK GDPR lets an organisation process personal data where it is necessary for its
legitimate interests, provided those interests are not overridden by the interests
or rights of the person concerned (Article 6(1)(f)). The regulator expects that
judgement to be **written down before the processing starts**, not reconstructed
afterwards. This is that record.

It covers only business outreach. Donor data, Gift Aid records and contact-form
messages are covered by the privacy notice at nbcc.scot/privacy and are not in
scope here.

---

## 1. What we actually do

A volunteer records a local business: its name, and where we have them, a contact
person's name, an email address and a phone number. The volunteer records **where
those details came from**, and a private note on why this business is worth
approaching.

We then send **one** email asking whether they would consider supporting us
monthly. It carries a link to a short booklet and to our donate page. If they do
not reply, one follow-up may be prepared for a volunteer to review and send. That
is the whole activity.

## 2. Whose personal data is involved

The business itself is not a person and is outside data-protection law. But a
named contact and a personal work email address (`jane@ayrjoinery.co.uk`) **are**
personal data, and a sole trader's business details are personal data throughout.
We have treated all of it as personal data.

---

## 3. The purpose test — is there a legitimate interest?

**Our interest.** We are a volunteer-run Scottish charity providing school
clothing, crisis support and Christmas gifts to children, young people and
vulnerable adults across South West Scotland. Regular monthly income lets us plan
what we can commit to rather than guess. Local business support is a normal and
long-established way for a charity of our size to find it.

**Whose interest is it?** Ours, and the community's. Every pound raised goes to
beneficiaries in the same towns as the businesses we write to. There is a
plausible benefit to the business too — supporters are listed on our website,
receive a certificate, and are thanked publicly if they wish — but we do not rely
on that to justify the processing.

**Is it lawful, and does it meet fundraising standards?** Yes. We follow the Code
of Fundraising Practice. Every email carries the charity's full name, its status
as a Scottish Charitable Incorporated Organisation, and charity number SC047995,
as the Charities and Benevolent Fundraising (Scotland) Regulations 2009 require.
We make no claim that a given sum buys a given outcome.

**How important is it?** Not trivial and not existential. It is one of several
income routes. Losing it would reduce what we can commit to in advance.

## 4. The necessity test — do we need the data to do it?

Yes, and only this data. To ask a business whether it would support us, we need to
know which business, and how to reach it.

- **Contact name** — optional. Where we have it, the email opens "Hello Jane"
  rather than "Hello", which is the difference between a letter and a mailshot.
- **Email address** — how the ask is delivered.
- **Phone number** — optional; used only where the business has engaged and a
  call is the sensible next step.
- **Where the details came from** — recorded so we can tell the business
  truthfully, and so we can show our working.

**Is there a less intrusive way?** We considered three. Advertising and waiting to
be approached does not work at our scale. Approaching only businesses we already
know limits us to a handful. Using a bought marketing list would involve *more*
data, from a source we could not vouch for, and we have ruled it out — we take
details only from a business's own website, a public business listing, its own
social media page, someone who passed them on, or the business itself.

**Is it proportionate?** One short email to a business address, with an
opt-out in the first paragraph and a real person's name at the bottom.

## 5. The balancing test — does our interest override theirs?

**What is the relationship?** None, at the point of first contact. This is a cold
approach, and we have weighted the balance accordingly.

**Would they expect it?** A business that publishes a contact address on its own
website expects to be contacted about business matters, and a local charity asking
for support is within that expectation. A sole trader has a stronger claim to be
left alone, which is why we treat them differently — see below.

**What is the impact on them?** Low. One email they can ignore, reply to, or ask
us to stop. No profiling, no automated decisions, no sharing with anyone, no
selling, no tracking across the internet. The link in our email carries a short
code so we can tell which approach produced a donation; it is our own reference,
it identifies the *email*, not the person, and no third party sees it.

**Is any of it sensitive?** No special-category data. No children's data.

**Could they reasonably object?** Yes, and we make it easy. Every email says, in
the body and not in small print, that a reply asking us to stop is enough. The
system then records that permanently, so nobody here writes to them again by
mistake — including a different volunteer years later.

**Where the balance tips against us — and what we do about it**

| Concern | What we do |
|---|---|
| They did not ask to hear from us | We tell them, in the email, where we got their details and link the privacy notice — Article 14, discharged at first contact rather than left to a page nobody visits |
| A sole trader is a person, not a company | Under PECR they are an "individual subscriber". We do **not** email one unless a volunteer has recorded how they already agreed to hear from us. Without that, the system refuses the send and offers a call or a letter instead |
| Being contacted repeatedly | One email, and at most one prepared follow-up. Every business is checked against everyone we have already contacted, everyone who already gives to us, and everyone who has declined, before it can even be added |
| An unwanted phone call | A business on the Corporate TPS register must not be called. Before a number is shown, the volunteer confirms they have checked the register, and that confirmation is recorded with their name and the date |
| Us holding their details indefinitely | See retention below |

## 6. Retention

A record with no activity for **three years** loses the personal parts: the
contact person's name, their phone number, the first half of their email address,
and the volunteer's free-text notes.

What remains is the business name, the email **domain**, the outcome, the dates,
and — permanently — any request not to be contacted. Keeping that much is
necessary: it is the only way to honour "do not contact us again" years later, and
the domain is what stops us writing to a different person at a firm that has
already said no. It holds no individual's personal details.

Records for businesses that are active supporters are governed by the donor
retention rules, not by this.

## 7. Their rights, and how they are met

| Right | How |
|---|---|
| To be informed | Stated in the first email and in the privacy notice |
| Of access | One click in the admin produces everything held about a business, including the volunteers' private notes |
| To rectification | A volunteer can correct any field |
| To erasure | On request. We keep only the business name and the do-not-contact fact, and we tell them so |
| To object | Explicitly offered in every email. Acting on it is immediate and permanent |

Volunteers are told, on the screen where they type them, to write notes as though
the business will one day read them — because on request, it will.

## 8. Records we keep

Every send, every outcome, every note and every change is recorded with the
volunteer's name and the time, in the admin audit trail. If we are ever asked to
account for how a business's details were used, the answer is retrievable rather
than remembered.

---

## Conclusion

We are satisfied that contacting local businesses about supporting the charity is
a legitimate interest, that the personal data used is the minimum needed, and that
the impact on the people concerned is low and adequately mitigated — in
particular by telling them at first contact where their details came from, by
making objection easy and permanent, by refusing to email sole traders without
their agreement, and by removing personal details after three years of silence.

**Legitimate interests is the appropriate lawful basis for this activity.**

We will review this assessment annually, and immediately if the activity changes —
for example if we ever bought a list, contacted businesses outside our area, or
began sending more than one follow-up. On the current evidence we would not expect
any of those to survive this test unchanged.
